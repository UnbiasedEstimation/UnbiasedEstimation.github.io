Formulating Marketing Impacts Precisely Is More Challenging Than It Appears

Author

Oliver Brose

Published

April 5, 2026

Data Science: formulating marketing impacts precisely (in the mathematical sense) is more challenging than it appears!

The causal impact of advertising, pricing, or other changes is typically described by comparing counterfactual scenarios. For example, to calculate the incremental ROAS for a channel, one first needs to derive how many sales would occur with a certain spending level and without it.

Many econometric papers focus on “average” effects and consider binary treatments. For example, many synthetic control papers consider cases with two potential outcomes — one with the intervention and one without.

This approach is intuitive and a great start, but it is not always flexible enough to accurately formulate marketing effects.

For example, a 30-day geotest to estimate paid search effectiveness — using selected sets of test and control regions — does not present only two scenarios. The reason is that budgets can be set every day, which leads to many possible combinations. Control regions might have zero spending for all 30 days, which can be represented by a vector of 30 zeros: (0, …, 0). Test regions might receive 10k on the first day, 15k on the second, and so on, which might be represented by another vector: (10k, 15k, …).

These vectors are sometimes called treatment histories [1].

A 30-day geotest can have many different treatment histories:

“It is clear from the definition that there are, in fact, many different (treatment effects): one for each pair of treatment histories. As the length of time of study grows, so does the number of possible comparisons.” [1]

On top of that we also have to consider lagged effects: if we spend today, we are likely to see effects in the following days.

Knowing how to formulate such effects precisely can be important in some cases. For example, consider geotesting for paid search again. Typically, companies have their paid search always on. In this scenario, a geotest requires shutting down advertising in selected test regions for a period of time. In the control regions, the standard advertising mix is maintained for comparison.

If paid search has lagged effects, activities before the experiment in the test regions can still influence KPIs during the experiment. Then the geotest does not necessarily test what we want — namely, comparing “some advertising” (control units) to “no advertising” (test units). Instead, the pre-experimental activities become mixed with the experiment.

One potential solution is to omit the first few days of the geotest — when lagged effects are expected — from the effect estimation. But decisions like this can be debatable. Knowing how to formulate treatment effects is a good basis for such decisions.

The first two sections in [1] are a great introduction on treatment effects in the dynamic scenario. There’s also a great Google paper [2], in particular section 4, that uses these ideas to formulate the ROAS. (That’s where the screenshot comes from.)
