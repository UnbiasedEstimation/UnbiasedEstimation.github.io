Why Warren Buffett’s investment philosophy is helpful in A/B testing too!

When we invest in stocks, we want to make money. Naturally, we try to focus on picking the “right” stocks that increase (hopefully soon). In A/B testing, we often think similarly, gain-oriented: we come up with some ideas about how a change can improve the business.

The problem? Just as stocks can not only go up but also down, A/B test ideas that should improve the business can actually hurt it.

Focus on gains is important, but focusing on limiting losses should not be neglected:

“The first rule of an investment is don’t lose [money]. And the second rule of an investment is don’t forget the first rule. And that’s all the rules there are.” — Warren Buffett

Why does Warren Buffett, one of the most successful investors of all time, say that? Here is some math. Suppose you invest 50k USD in some stocks. Suppose they lose 50% of their value, you panic, sell them, and end up with 25k. If you want to get back to the original 50k by investing in stocks again, you now have to double your investment. This is really hard … and even if you make it, you are basically just where you started!

This is why limiting risks is important.

Now, many people are frustrated with their A/B testing because they don’t get enough “significant” results. This often results in aggressive “searching for significance”: lowering the significance level, selectively removing outliers, running the test “until it reaches significance,” selectively searching for subgroups where “the result is significant”, and so on.

What is the consequence? Consider a simple A/B test scenario, where variant A is the baseline and variant B implements our idea. Suppose we strongly wish that B > A. If this wish is indeed true, the described aggressive significance searching can actually help to make the right decision! But if our wish is wrong, it can increase the chance of incorrectly concluding that our idea B improves the business (B > A), even though its implementation hurts the business (since A > B).

Even if we now find good ideas C, D, E, etc., we first still need to pay for the losses of our incorrect previous test result … just to get to the point where we started!

This is especially relevant if you try to improve something with A/B testing that already works close to optimum. For example, consider optimizing email marketing in a mature company. Suppose that the email marketing is already close to optimum with respect to revenue (or profit) per subscriber, and the goal is to further improve it by testing new ideas. Only a few ideas might improve the business, but many more can hurt it. Here, A/B testing can rarely deliver wins, because they are rare.

The key value here is limiting the implementation of ideas that make the business worse.
